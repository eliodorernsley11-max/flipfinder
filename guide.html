Your First Wholesale Deal

Put a property under contract, assign it to a cash buyer, and get paid an assignment fee — without buying the house. Here's the honest, step-by-step way to do it.

The process, step by step

Do these in order. Most first-deal problems come from skipping step 1 or step 6.

Check your state's rules

Some states require a real estate license to market contracts you don't own, or require written disclosure to the seller and buyer. Look up your state's rules (or ask a local real estate attorney) before you make a single offer.

Find motivated sellers

Look for pre-foreclosures, inherited or vacant homes, tired landlords, tax-delinquent and code-violation properties. Contact owners directly, and stay compliant with Do-Not-Call and texting-consent laws.

Run the numbers with the 70% rule

Estimate the after-repair value (ARV) from recent nearby sold comps, estimate repairs, then work out your maximum allowable offer.

MAO = ARV × 0.70 − repairs − your fee

Example: $150,000 × 0.70 − $30,000 − $10,000 = $65,000.

Sign the purchase agreement

Use a Purchase and Sale Agreement naming the buyer as "Your Company and/or assigns," with an assignment clause, an inspection contingency of 10–14 days (your legitimate exit), and a small refundable earnest money deposit held by the title company.

Open escrow with an assignment-friendly title company

Call ahead and confirm they close assignments (and double closes). Send them the signed contract right away so title work starts early.

Build your cash buyers list

Ideally before you contract. Find landlords and flippers through recent cash sales in county records, local investor meetups, and property managers. Ask what areas, prices and property types they buy.

Market the deal with honest numbers

Send a deal sheet with the address, photos or video, ARV backed by real comps, a realistic repair estimate, your price, and closing date. Say clearly that you're selling an assignment of contract.

Sign the assignment

Use an Assignment of Contract stating the assignment fee and requiring a non-refundable deposit from the cash buyer, paid to the title company. Ask for proof of funds first.

Close and get paid

The title company collects the buyer's funds, pays the seller the contract price, and pays your assignment fee on the closing statement. Keep copies of everything.

Do's & Don'ts

Wholesaling has a reputation problem because of people who skip these. Don't be one of them.

Do

  • Be honest with sellers that you intend to assign the contract
  • Verify comps yourself — don't trust a single automated estimate
  • Walk the property, or at least get a video walkthrough
  • Confirm your title company handles assignments early
  • Put the inspection deadline on your calendar
  • Build your buyers list before you contract
  • Get an attorney to review your contracts the first time
  • Track every lead, offer, deadline and document

Don't

  • Market a property you don't have under contract
  • Misrepresent yourself as the buyer who will close, or as an agent
  • Inflate the ARV to cash buyers
  • Put down a big non-refundable earnest money deposit
  • Miss contract deadlines
  • Ignore your state's licensing laws
  • Sign sheriff-auction or "no assignment" deals expecting to assign them
  • Pressure distressed sellers
  • Cold-text without consent or ignore Do-Not-Call rules

Glossary

The words you'll hear on every deal.

ARV
After-Repair Value — what the home should sell for once fixed up, based on recent comparable sales.
MAO
Maximum Allowable Offer — the most you can offer and still leave room for your buyer's profit and your fee.
EMD
Earnest Money Deposit — good-faith money held by the title company; keep yours small and refundable during inspection.
Assignment fee
What you're paid for transferring your contract: the cash buyer's price minus your contract price.
Double close
You actually buy and immediately resell the same day in two closings — used when assignment isn't allowed. Needs funding.
Inspection contingency
A period (often 10–14 days) to inspect and cancel if not satisfied, with your EMD refunded.
Title company
Neutral party that checks ownership and liens, holds deposits, and handles the closing and payouts.
Comps
Comparable recently sold homes nearby (similar size, age, condition) used to estimate value.
